Teaching script · neurosurgery residents

Craniocervical Junction:
10-Minute Chalk Talk

Source: Vigo et al., Immersive Surgical Anatomy of the Craniocervical Junction, Cureus 2020.

0:00–0:45

Hook

Open the presentation in fullscreen. Ask:

“How do you move from the surface of the CCJ to the neuraxis without losing the artery, nerve, or stabilizing structures?”

Set the frame: this is a layered, mobile 3D unit—not a flat list of landmarks.

0:45–2:00

Orient the unit

Use the layered model. Have residents identify the occiput, C1 ring, C2, foramen magnum, and dens. Emphasize that the CCJ combines high mobility with a tight neurovascular envelope.

2:00–3:15

Build the bony map

Move through the occiput, atlas, and axis models. Ask residents to name the condyles, C1 posterior arch, transverse foramen, and dens. Pause at the C1 posterior arch: the V3 groove and C1 dorsal ramus are the first danger-zone relationship to retain.

3:15–4:30

Add the stabilizers

Use the ligament model. Ask:

  • What holds the dens against the anterior arch? — The transverse ligament.
  • What checks excessive rotation? — The alar ligaments.
  • What spans the dens toward the skull base? — The tectorial membrane and related ligamentous envelope.
4:30–5:45

Use the muscle layers as a map

Show the suboccipital triangle. Have residents name its boundaries before its contents. Reinforce the sequence: muscle boundary → V3 artery → C1 nerve → bony landmark.

5:45–7:00

Trace the neurovascular corridor

Use the neurovascular figure and model. Ask residents to trace V1–V4 and identify where V3 turns around C1 and where V4 becomes intradural. Mention that the course is variable and must be checked on patient-specific imaging.

7:00–8:15

Make imaging answer a question

Show the morphometrics slide. Group measurements into settling, foramen magnum relationships, clivus–canal geometry, alignment, and instability. Do not ask residents to memorize every number; ask which measurement answers the surgical question.

8:15–9:15

Turn anatomy into approach geometry

Use the surgical-lines model. Ask where a target lies relative to the hard-palate and nasal / dental-arch lines. Emphasize that approach selection is trajectory geometry, not diagnosis alone.

9:15–10:00

Close with the five

Have residents recite:

  1. Bone
  2. Ligament
  3. Artery
  4. Nerve
  5. Geometry

End with: “Before you operate, name the five.”

Optional extension: use the case-based quiz for another 5–10 minutes, or assign it asynchronously after residents explore the models.
